Abstract

Blockchain-based academic credential verification for African higher education

The integrity of academic credentials is a growing concern for institutions across Africa, where traditional verification methods are often manual, fragmented, and vulnerable to fraud. TrustedCert explores a blockchain-based academic credential verification system tailored to African higher education, integrating smart contracts, IPFS, and Ethereum to securely issue, revoke, and verify credentials.

The prototype was tested on the Ethereum Sepolia testnet using simulated data. The findings show that blockchain can help curb credential fraud, reduce administrative burden, and improve international trust in African academic qualifications.

Background

The credential trust problem

Academic fraud undermines institutional credibility, graduate employability, and confidence in cross-border qualification checks. Manual verification through physical certificates or direct institutional correspondence is slow, expensive, and vulnerable to tampering.

TrustedCert responds to this gap with a decentralized verification model where approved institutions issue certificate records, documents are stored with content identifiers, and employers or admissions teams can verify authenticity without private back-channel requests.

Problem Statement

Existing platforms are often centralized, costly, or hard to adapt

Many current digital credentialing systems depend on centralized vendors, closed hosting models, or technical deployment paths that are difficult for low-resource institutions to maintain. TrustedCert is designed as a modular, open-source, smart-contract-based alternative that can be adapted for regional education systems.

Objectives

What the system sets out to prove

  • Integrate Ethereum smart contracts and decentralized storage for issuing, revoking, and verifying academic credentials.
  • Enforce security and integrity requirements through role-based issuance and revocation.
  • Evaluate prototype performance and transaction behavior under controlled workloads.
  • Compare TrustedCert with existing blockchain credential architectures.
  • Assess usability, trustworthiness, and adoption feasibility for higher-education stakeholders.

Methodology

Agile development with design-based evaluation

The project followed an iterative development approach informed by prior blockchain credentialing systems and education-policy research. The smart contract was developed with Solidity and OpenZeppelin, tested with Hardhat, and deployed to Sepolia. The frontend uses JavaScript and Ethers.js, while certificate files are pinned to IPFS through a secure backend upload proxy.

System Architecture

A modular trust stack

Smart contractHandles institution roles, certificate issuance, revocation, verification, and pause controls.

StorageUses IPFS through Pinata to store certificate files by content identifier.

FrontendProvides public verification, institution issuance, and admin controls through HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and Ethers.js.

BackendServes public configuration and protects private IPFS upload credentials.

Wallet integrationUses MetaMask for account connection, role checks, and transaction signing.

Testing

Validation across roles and workflows

Testing covered role management, certificate registration, duplicate prevention, revocation, verification of valid and invalid records, pause behavior, and end-to-end integration flows. The smart contract tests confirmed that only authorized wallets could issue or revoke certificates and that revoked or non-existent certificates were handled correctly.

Conclusion

A practical foundation for tamper-resistant academic records

TrustedCert demonstrates how blockchain, smart contracts, and decentralized storage can work together to improve credential authenticity, support cross-border verification, and reduce reliance on manual institutional checks. The project is positioned as a replicable foundation for future institutional pilots and production-grade credential networks.
